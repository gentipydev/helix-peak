## Experience

The zoom (`/zoom/<slug>`) dives from a whole body to the first bases of one
protein's gene, through nine stops: body, organ, tissue, cell, nucleus,
chromosome, band, gene and DNA. It is one continuous depth.

- **Getting there.** The walk's gene page offers "Zoom ›" beside its line,
  where the transcript offers "Ribosome ›", for a protein whose `locus`
  track is ready: the twenty curated ones today. It began as a Lab flow, and
  its links from then (`/lab/zoom/<slug>`) still open it.
- **Moving through it.**
  - The card's ‹ and › fly to the stop either side, and a double tap goes
    one stop deeper. A step asked for mid-flight carries straight on at the
    speed the depth has.
  - A pinch moves the depth 2.5 for each tenfold spread of the fingers. A
    flick settles on the stop it would coast to, on a critically damped
    spring.
  - There is no player and no depth rail: the buttons and the pinch are
    the whole of it.
- **The card** holds the stop's place, one line of data where there is one,
  and its two steps (`‹ Organ`, `Cell ›`; `Walk ›` at the DNA). Nothing on
  it explains. A new stop's words slide 6 pt the way the zoom travels and
  fade in, in 220 ms. The card is as tall as the tallest stop's words at the
  reader's text size, so the canvas keeps its size. A screen reader hears
  the stop's name, its place and its line.
- **The canvas** names only what the card does not say: the cell in the
  tissue, where the protein sits, the band on the chromosome, the gene on
  the map, the gene's 5′ end; and the landmarks a reader asked after: the
  nucleolus, at the nucleus (the user's ask, 2026-10-19; named at the cell
  from 2026-10-15 to 2026-10-18, and at the nucleus too until 2026-10-16),
  and the chromosome's parts, at the chromosome (the same day). A
  scale bar is always at its lower left: a
  round length at each stop, and between two stops a bar that glides from
  the one's length to the next, reading the length it spans as it goes, so
  it never springs back. At the body alone, its lower right carries the
  credit, "Anatomogram EMBL-EBI · HPA 25.1 · CC BY 4.0", built from the
  track's Atlas version and the figures' licence. It goes onto two lines
  where it would meet the scale bar, and fades as the body does.
- **Reduced motion.** Steps and settles cut, the cards cut, and every stop
  is drawn as its beats leave it, with nothing moving.
- **At the DNA**, "Walk ›" unzips the helix into the walk's rows and goes
  back to the walk's gene page it was opened from. A zoom opened by a link,
  with no walk under it, opens the walk there.

## Research and its consequences

Each stop is laid out in the convention of the instrument that shows its
scale, so a reader who knows the field reads it without a legend. What each
convention is, where it comes from, and what it decided:

- **Where a gene is read.** The Human Protein Atlas reads a gene's RNA
  tissue by tissue, cell type by cell type, and by cell type within a tissue,
  and finds its protein in the cell by immunofluorescence (Uhlén et al. 2015;
  Karlsson et al. 2021; Thul et al. 2017). The tissue reading and the single
  cell reading are separate analyses, so the top of one need not live in the
  top of the other. *Consequence:* the bake chooses one path, a cell that
  lives in the organ ("One path per gene").
- **Anatomy.** Expression Atlas shows expression on an anatomogram: a
  standing figure with a shape for each tissue, named by its UBERON id.
  *Consequence:* the body and the organ are those shapes, glowing by the
  Atlas's level.
- **The pancreas.** Anatomy plates show it from the front with the stomach
  taken away: its head in the C of the duodenum, the uncinate process
  hooked behind the superior mesenteric vessels, the neck over them, the
  body across the spine and the tail at the spleen's hilum; the splenic
  artery winding along its upper border from the coeliac trunk; the bile
  duct coming down behind the duodenum's first part and the head to the
  ampulla on the second part, where the main duct meets it, the accessory
  duct opening above (Standring 2020). Its surface is lobulated. Its
  islets, a million or more, are each some 150 µm across, the size islet
  isolation counts them by (Ricordi et al. 1990), and lie more than twice
  as densely in the tail as in the head or the body (Wang et al. 2013).
  Its main duct narrows from about 3 mm in the head to 1 mm in the tail
  (Sivak and Sullivan 1976). *Consequence:* the pancreas is drawn so, from
  anatomy rather than from the anatomogram's outline ("The pancreas"
  below).
- **The testis.** Anatomy plates show it from the side and cut down its
  length: 4–5 cm long, 2.5 cm broad and 3 cm from front to back, its 250
  to 400 lobules parted by septa that run from the mediastinum, the
  thickened back of its tunica albuginea, to the tunica round it; the rete
  testis in the mediastinum, drained at its upper end by twelve to fifteen
  or twenty efferent ductules into the head of the epididymis, which lies
  along the back down to its tail (Gray 1918). Each lobule holds one to
  four coiled seminiferous tubules 150–250 µm across (Junqueira's Basic
  Histology). From the tail the vas deferens turns up into the spermatic
  cord beside the testicular artery and the veins of the pampiniform
  plexus. Measured in men: the epididymis's head up to 12 mm across on
  ultrasound and its tail up to 6 (Lotti et al. 2021); the vas 1.5–2.7 mm
  thick (Middleton et al. 2009); the testicular artery about 1 mm across in
  the cord, 0.2–1.9 mm in 120 cords (Raman and Goldstein 2004); a vein of
  the plexus 3 mm across or more is read as a varicocele (Bertolotto et al.
  2020). *Consequence:* the testis is drawn so, from anatomy rather than
  from the anatomogram's outline, at those sizes ("The testis" below).
- **The bone marrow.** Anatomy plates cut the femur down its length in the
  frontal plane, through its head, neck and shaft: the cortex thickest round
  the middle of the shaft and a shell at either end, cartilage over the
  head and the condyles, the scars of the growth plates, the cavity down
  the shaft, and in the ends the trabeculae of the spongy bone in the
  groups Koch traced (Gray 1918, after Koch 1917; the groups as Singh,
  Nagrath and Maini 1970 name them). The nutrient artery enters through a
  canal directed obliquely upward (Gray 1918), its foramen 29 to 69% of
  the femur's length from its top (Kizilkanat et al. 2007). Measured in
  men: the femur 449.7 ± 16.7 mm long, its head 47.0 ± 2.3 mm across, its
  shaft 27.9 ± 2.5 at its middle and its condyles 79.6 ± 4.6 (Kanz et al.
  2015); on CT the neck meets the shaft at 127.2 ± 5.2°, the head's centre
  lies 37.5 ± 4.6 mm from the shaft's axis and the cavity is 11.9 ± 1.7 mm
  across at its isthmus (Kulkarni et al. 2020), 10.71 ± 2.2 in 1,189
  femurs (Thiesen et al. 2022); the head's cartilage is 2.8 mm thick round
  the fovea and 1.1 to 1.3 toward its rims (Nakanishi et al. 2001), the
  condyles' 2.3 ± 0.5 (Sandhar et al. 2026). An adult's red marrow is in
  the upper end: the shaft turns to yellow marrow by ten and the lower end
  by twenty, the adult pattern set by 24 (Moore and Dawson 1990); the head
  and the greater trochanter are yellow in every femur, and 95% of femurs
  under fifty keep red marrow between the trochanters (Mitchell et al.
  1986), along the weight-bearing axis (Niu et al. 2007). *Consequence:*
  the bone marrow is a femur drawn so, from anatomy rather than from the
  anatomogram's outline, and the zoom goes into it in its upper end ("The
  bone marrow" below).
- **The salivary gland.** A dissection of the face shows the parotid from
  the side, below and in front of the ear, its front edge over the masseter,
  an accessory part detached below the zygomatic arch, the submandibular
  gland below the jaw (Gray 1918); its duct runs forward over the masseter
  and turns in at its front edge (Iwanaga et al. 2026), and the facial
  nerve's five branches leave its front edge and its lower pole, the buccal
  running along the duct in three sides of four (Sagkriotis et al. 2026).
  Measured: the superficial lobe a median 36 by 44 mm on ultrasound and the
  submandibular gland 35 by 23 (Fang et al. 2019); the duct 35 ± 8 mm to its
  bend, its lumen 0.8 to 2 mm (Avishai et al. 2022); an accessory gland on a
  tenth of sides, 15.8 by 5.0 mm (Ahn et al. 2017); the nerve's trunk 2.5 mm
  across (Khoa et al. 2019); the superficial temporal artery 1.88 mm at the
  arch (Nguyen et al. 2026) and its vein 1.3 (Nokovitch et al. 2021); the
  ear 66 by 28 mm (Cho et al. 2025). *Consequence:* the salivary gland is
  its parotid drawn so, in place ("The salivary gland" below).
- **The brain.** Its medial face is drawn over the MNI152 2009c average of
  152 adult brains, the anatomical fiducials raters placed on it (the
  commissures, the genu and the splenium, the pineal, the colliculi, the
  mammillary bodies, the culmen) taken unscaled (Lau et al. 2019), its sulci
  and arteries after the atlases of each (Ono et al. 1990; Duvernoy 1999)
  and Gray's Anatomy (Standring 2020). On MRI a pons is 23 mm deep and a
  medulla 13 (Singh et al. 2019). *Consequence:* the brain is drawn so, cut
  down its middle ("The brain" below).
- **The eye.** Gray's and Netter's plates cut it level through its optic
  nerve. Measured: the globe about 24 mm (Gray 1918); the cornea 11.71 mm
  across (Rüfer et al. 2005) and 0.52 thick in the middle (Maurice 1969),
  the anterior chamber 3.05 deep and the lens's curves as Le Grand's
  schematic eye has them (Le Grand and El Hage 1980); the lens 9.21 by 4.18
  mm (Xue and Zou 2019); the iris 0.45 to 0.60 mm (Wang et al. 2011); the
  sclera 0.39 to 1.0 mm (Olsen et al. 1998); the choroid 0.287 mm under the
  fovea (Margolis and Spaide 2009); the retina 0.32 mm at the fovea's rim
  and 0.08 at the ora serrata, the fovea 1.5 mm across and 3.4 mm from the
  disc's edge (Yamada 1969; Polyak 1941, as Webvision gathers them); the
  optic nerve 2.76 mm across 3 mm behind the globe and its sheath 5.10
  (Watcharakorn et al. 2021), its central artery piercing the sheath about
  10 mm behind (Singh and Dass 1960); the medial and lateral recti inserting
  5.5 and 6.9 mm behind the limbus, their bellies 4.1 and 3.4 mm thick (Rana
  et al. 2022). *Consequence:* the eye is drawn so, cut through its nerve
  ("The eye" below).
- **The heart.** Every textbook cuts it open from front to back to show the
  four chambers, the valves and the great vessels (Gray 1918; OpenStax 2022,
  fig. 19.9), a composite that no one plane holds. Measured in men in
  diastole: the left ventricle 52 ± 5 mm across, its wall 8.3 mm at the base
  and 5.6 at the apex, the septum 9.0, the atria 58 by 43 and 53 by 48 mm,
  the aortic annulus 23 and the pulmonary trunk 23 (Kawel-Boehm et al.
  2020); the right ventricle's free wall 3 to 5 mm (Lang et al. 2015); the
  mitral annulus about 31 mm across (Nemes et al. 2025) and the tricuspid 33
  ± 4 (Muraru et al. 2022); the pulmonary veins 9 to 13 mm wide (Kim et al.
  2005); the inferior vena cava 21 at most (Rudski et al. 2010); the
  coronaries 2.8 to 4.2 mm near their origins (Dodge et al. 1992). GTEx
  takes heart muscle from the anterior left ventricle (its tissue
  procurement SOP). *Consequence:* heart muscle is the heart drawn so, gone
  into in its left ventricle's wall ("The heart" below).
- **Skeletal muscle.** The plates draw the front of the thigh from the iliac
  spines to the knee (Netter; Gray 1918, fig. 430). Its muscles were
  measured in 21 limbs from people the figure's height: the rectus femoris
  36.3 cm long, the sartorius 44.8 and the adductor longus 21.8, with their
  fibres' angles (Ward et al. 2009). Human muscle is biopsied from the
  vastus lateralis with the Bergström needle (Shanely et al. 2014), best
  between half and three quarters of the way down it, just in front of the
  iliotibial tract (Chen et al. 2019). *Consequence:* skeletal muscle is the
  front of the thigh drawn so, gone into in its vastus lateralis there
  ("Skeletal muscle" below).
- **The kidney.** Every plate cuts it open from front to back: the cortex
  and its columns, the pyramids and their papillae, the calyces and the
  pelvis in the sinus, the vessels at the hilum. Measured: 12 by 6 cm in the
  textbooks (Alyami et al. 2024) and 10.7 cm long on ultrasound, its cortex
  1.1 cm thick (El-Reshaid and Abdul-Fattah 2014); 21 pyramids on average
  (Gregory et al. 2023); some 918,000 glomeruli (Hughson et al. 2014), each
  about 0.23 mm across (Sasaki et al. 2018), about two to a square
  millimetre of a cut (Sasaki et al. 2019); the thick ascending limb of the
  loop of Henle running up through the outer medulla and the medullary rays
  (Lake et al. 2023), its cells making most of the uromodulin (Karagiannidis
  et al. 2024); the renal artery 5.5 mm across (Abd Elrahim 2020). A kidney
  is biopsied at its lower pole, through its cortex (Granata et al. 2021).
  *Consequence:* the kidney is drawn so, and gone into in its lower pole's
  cortex ("The kidney" below).
- **The liver.** It is read as a coronal CT reads it: the vena cava behind
  the caudate lobe, the hepatic veins fanning out of it, the portal vein
  dividing at the porta, its branches in Glisson's sheaths, the gallbladder
  in its fossa, the round ligament parting the lobes (Marcinkevičiūtė et al.
  2026; Gray 1918). Measured: 199 by 150 mm in fifty cadavers (Gupta, Sodhi
  and Yadav 2008) and 14.0 cm long in the midclavicular line in 2,080 adults
  (Kratzer et al. 2003); the portal vein 10.79 mm (Ayele et al. 2022), the
  right hepatic vein 8.97 (Gündoğdu 2023), the vena cava 21 at most (Rudski
  et al. 2010), the bile duct 3.1 to 4.4 (Hela and Maitra 2025), the
  gallbladder 7 to 10 cm long (Gray 1918). *Consequence:* the liver is drawn
  so, a composite of its cut ("The liver" below).
- **The stomach.** The plates open it from the front: the fundus, the body,
  the antrum and the pyloric canal, the rugae for the most part lengthwise,
  the pylorus a ring of its circular muscle, the arteries along its two
  curvatures (Gray 1918). Its wall is 5.1 ± 1.1 mm on ultrasound (Rapaccini
  et al. 1988) and 3.27 on endoscopic ultrasound (Larsen et al. 2011),
  thickest in the antrum and thinnest in the fundus in resected stomachs
  (Endo et al. 2025); the left gastric artery is 2.7 mm across and the right
  1.6 (Tuan et al. 2026), the right gastroepiploic 2.49 at its origin
  (Howell et al. 2018). *Consequence:* the stomach is drawn so, opened from
  the front ("The stomach" below).
- **The esophagus.** Gray (1918) gives its course, 23 to 25 cm from the
  cricoid to the cardia, its curves, its constrictions and its abdominal
  part, and its four coats. Its wall is 4 to 5.7 mm thick where it is
  contracted and 1.9 to 2.7 where it is distended, on CT (Xia et al. 2009);
  its muscle is thickest at the gastroesophageal ring (Liebermann-Meffert et
  al. 1979); the cricopharyngeus is the upper sphincter's lower third
  (Sivarao and Goyal 2000); the aorta beside it is 2.4 to 2.6 cm across
  (Hager et al. 2002). GTEx takes its mucosa in its lower third, at least 4
  cm above the gastroesophageal junction (GTEx Portal). *Consequence:* the
  esophagus is drawn so, opened from the front down its length, and gone
  into in its lower third ("The esophagus" below).
- **The skin.** GTEx takes sun-exposed skin 2 cm below the knee, on the
  leg's inner side (GTEx Portal). Measured: the epidermis 56.6 to 81.5 µm
  (Sandby-Møller et al. 2003), 76.5 pooled (Xu et al. 2017); the calf's
  dermis 1.72 mm and its whole skin 1.83 (Wang et al. 2025); 57 eccrine
  glands to the square centimetre of the leg, each coil about 3.5 mm of tube
  40 µm across (Taylor and Machado-Moreira 2013); two vascular plexuses
  joined by paired vessels (Braverman 1997); melanocytes in the basal layer,
  955 to the square millimetre (Xu et al. 2017), and in the hair bulb
  (Slominski et al. 2005); the calf's follicles the fewest of seven sites
  (Otberg et al. 2004); the follicle, its sebaceous gland and its arrector
  pili, and the sweat glands, as Gray (1918) describes them, his Pacinian
  corpuscles only on the palm, the sole and the genitals. *Consequence:* the
  skin is a block of it cut there ("The skin" below).
- **The thyroid.** It is seen from the front in place: each lobe about 5 cm
  long, the isthmus over the second and third tracheal rings, the carotids
  and the jugulars at its sides; cut, it is brownish red, of vesicles parted
  by septa from its capsule (Gray 1918). In 132 women it held 17.5 ml
  (Hegedüs et al. 1983), so its lobes are drawn 20 mm across; the common
  carotid is 6.10 mm and the internal 4.66 (Krejza et al. 2006); the
  trachea's lumen 14.8 mm (Zahedi-Nejad et al. 2011); the thyroid
  cartilage's laminae 27 mm tall (Vadgaonkar et al. 2024). A pyramidal lobe
  rises in 42.8% of people (Ostrowski et al. 2023), less than half, so none
  is drawn. *Consequence:* the thyroid is drawn so, its right lobe cut open
  ("The thyroid" below).
- **The adrenal.** Each gland lies above and in front of its kidney's upper
  end, 3 to 5 cm long and 4 to 6 mm thick, the right roughly triangular, its
  vein to the vena cava, the left crescentic, its vein to the renal vein;
  cut, the cortex is deep yellow and the medulla dark (Gray 1918). On CT the
  right gland's body is 0.61 cm across and the left's 0.79, their limbs
  about 0.3 (Vincent et al. 1994). *Consequence:* the adrenal is drawn so,
  each side of its own shape, cut through its thickness ("The adrenal"
  below).
- **The pituitary.** It is about 1 cm across in its fossa, its anterior lobe
  kidney-shaped round the posterior, the pars intermedia a thin lamina
  between them, the chiasm in front of its stalk (Gray 1918), and 5.78 mm
  tall and 10.25 long on MRI (Saba et al. 2017); the brain's own lobes, on
  its template (Lau et al. 2019), are 7.2 by 11.2. *Consequence:* the
  pituitary is drawn cut down its middle in the brain's own coordinates, so
  the two stops agree ("The pituitary" below).
- **The lymph node.** It is a bean with a hilum, afferent vessels all round
  it, its cortex lighter than its medulla, trabeculae from its capsule and a
  sinus under it, follicles in the cortex and cords in the medulla (Gray
  1918); cortex, paracortex and medulla, B cells in the follicles and T
  cells in the paracortex (Willard-Mack 2006). A normal node is under 10 mm
  across its short axis and oval, its long axis more than twice its short
  (Möller et al. 2026). *Consequence:* the node is drawn so, 18 by 8.4 mm,
  cut through its hilum ("The lymph node" below).
- **The fallopian tube.** It is about 10 cm long: its isthmus, its ampulla
  arching over the ovary, its infundibulum ringed by fimbriae, one of them
  attached to the ovary; its mucosa in longitudinal folds, far more
  extensive in the ampulla than in the isthmus, ciliated, as on the
  fimbriae's inner faces (Gray 1918). *Consequence:* the tube is drawn so,
  along the anatomogram's own course, opened along its length ("The
  fallopian tube" below).
- **The placenta.** Its villi branch in the intervillous space, bathed in
  maternal blood, some anchored to the basal plate; septa rise from the
  basal plate between its lobules and never reach the chorionic plate; the
  cord's vessels radiate under the amnion (Gray 1918; Quaye et al. 2026). It
  is 39.9 mm thick in utero at 40 weeks (Ali et al. 2026) and 1.72 cm once
  delivered (Park et al. 2018); a converted spiral artery is 0.4 to 0.5 mm
  across, its mouth 2.4 mm, opening into a lobule's villus-free cavity, the
  lobules 2 to 3 cm (Burton et al. 2009); the cord is 186 mm² in section
  (Rostamzadeh et al. 2015) and coils 0.34 times a centimetre (Celik et al.
  2026); the uterine wall is 6.3 to 6.9 mm at 34 weeks (Louwagie et al.
  2021), thicker behind the placenta (Degani et al. 1998). *Consequence:*
  the placenta is a block cut through its whole thickness in its uterine
  wall ("The placenta" below).
- **The fat.** The abdomen's subcutaneous tissue has a superficial layer of
  round lobes between vertical septa, a membranous layer, and a deep layer
  of large flat lobes between oblique septa (Lancerotto et al. 2011; Markman
  and Barton 1987; Fede et al. 2025). In 300 women of normal weight it is
  17.48 mm thick by ultrasound, its superficial layer 8.18 (Annunziata et
  al. 2025), and the skin over it 2.29 mm (Sim et al. 2014); perforators of
  the deep inferior epigastric artery reach the skin through the rectus
  (Childs et al. 2023). Abdominal fat is biopsied at the navel's level 4 to
  8 cm beside it (Dineen et al. 2023; Lynch et al. 2024; Lempesis et al.
  2024). *Consequence:* adipose tissue is a block of the abdominal wall cut
  5 cm beside the navel ("The fat" below).
- **The bladder.** Anatomy plates open it from the front (Gray 1918, Fig.
  1140). Moderately full it holds about half a litre and is an oval about
  12 cm long. Its mucosa, "of a pale rose color", is loosely attached to
  the muscle and folded when it is contracted, the folds effaced when it is
  distended; over the trigone it is bound to the muscle and always smooth.
  The trigone's angles are the internal urethral orifice, somewhat
  crescentic, in the most dependent part, and the ureters' orifices,
  slit-like, about 2.5 cm apart and as far from the urethral orifice when
  it is contracted, up to about 5 cm when it is distended. Its base is the
  torus uretericus, a slightly curved ridge that shows as a pale band and
  runs on past the orifices as the plicae uretericae, raised by the ureters
  in their 2 cm oblique course through the wall. Its wall has four coats,
  the muscle's middle layer thickened round the urethral orifice into the
  sphincter vesicae; its arteries are the superior, middle and inferior
  vesical; the female urethra is about 4 cm long and 6 mm across (Gray
  1918). Measured in adults: the wall 2.0 ± 0.4 mm at 338 mL on ultrasound
  in 95 (Kanyilmaz et al. 2013), and on CT 1.9 mm in women whose bladder
  is 9 cm long or more (Feinberg et al. 2026); the detrusor 1.2 mm in women
  and 1.4 in men, the same from 250 mL on (Oelke et al. 2006); the lamina
  propria 0.46 to 3.07 mm deep in resected bladders (Paner et al. 2007).
  GTEx samples the central posterior wall (GTEx Portal). *Consequence:*
  the bladder is drawn so, part-filled and opened from the front, from
  anatomy rather than from the anatomogram's outline ("The bladder"
  below).
- **The breast.** Anatomy plates show it from the front, its lower half
  dissected down to the gland (Gray 1918, Fig. 1172, after Luschka). It
  extends from the second rib to the sixth and from the side of the sternum
  to near the mid-axillary line, its long axis up and out toward the
  axilla; the nipple lies just below the centre, about the fourth
  intercostal space, its smooth muscle circular round its base, on the
  areola, which bears the areolar glands. The gland is lobes of lobules,
  each lobe's ducts uniting in one that converges on the areola; it is
  thicker at the centre than at the edge and joined to the skin by Cooper's
  ligamenta suspensoria; fat fills the intervals between its lobes, but
  none lies beneath the areola and the nipple; its arteries come from the
  axillary's thoracic branches, the intercostals and the internal thoracic,
  and its lymph passes to the pectoral axillary nodes (Gray 1918). Modern
  counts give 5 to 9 orifices on the nipple, each a separate ductal system
  (Love and Barsky 2004), a median of 23 ducts in the nipple (Rusby et al.
  2007), and by ultrasound 9 main ducts 1.9 to 2.0 mm across, all branching
  within the areola's radius, the first 8 mm from the nipple, with no
  sinuses (Ramsay et al. 2005). A lobule spans a median 740 µm before the
  menopause (Wetstein et al. 2020); the areola is 4.5 to 5.1 cm across and
  the nipple 1.2 cm (Al-Qattan et al. 2019; Archibong et al. 2021); the
  skin 0.5 to 2.7 mm, thicker medially and below (Pope et al. 1984; Willson
  et al. 1982); the fascia under the nipple and the areola holds smooth
  muscle (Komiya et al. 2015); the internal thoracic artery reaches the
  nipple through the first to third intercostal spaces, the second most
  often (Thuy et al. 2025), its main branch at least 1 mm across (Hosny et
  al. 2025). GTEx samples the central subareolar region of the right
  breast, 1 to 2 cm under the nipple (GTEx Portal). *Consequence:* the
  breast is drawn so, a resting adult's, in place from the front, its lower
  half dissected ("The breast" below).
- **The cervix.** Anatomy plates show it opened, the uterus's back half and
  the vagina's upper part seen from the front (Gray 1918, Fig. 1167). The
  cervix is somewhat conical, slightly wider in the middle than above or
  below; the vagina's attachment round it divides it into a supravaginal
  and a vaginal portion, the vaginal one projecting free between the
  fornices, its external orifice small and somewhat circular between two
  lips. Its canal is fusiform, broader at its middle, from the internal
  orifice at the isthmus to the external; down each wall runs a ridge from
  which the palmate folds slant off "like branches from the stem of a
  tree"; the mucosa of its upper two-thirds has deep glandular follicles,
  and little cysts, the ovula Nabothi, lie along it; its columnar
  epithelium changes to stratified squamous close to the external orifice,
  and the vaginal surface is squamous. The uterus's muscle is "dense, firm,
  of a grayish color"; the vagina has a column down its back wall, with
  transverse rugae; the uterine artery reaches the cervix's margin, ascends
  tortuously beside the uterus and sends branches down the vagina, and
  crosses the ureter about 2 cm from the cervix (Gray 1918). Measured: the
  canal 33.95 mm long in 20 hysterectomy specimens (Vinnikov et al. 2024);
  the vagina widest just below the cervix, 32.5 mm, on MRI in 28 women
  (Barnhart et al. 2006); the deepest crypt of a cone 3.38 mm deep on
  average, in 343 (Anderson and Hartley 1980); the squamous epithelium
  246 ± 91 µm thick (Wang et al. 2021); Nabothian cysts formed at the
  squamocolumnar junction (Mikes and Puckett 2025); the ureter 2.3 ± 0.8 cm
  from the cervix's margin on CT (Hurd et al. 2001), 2.8 cm where the
  uterine artery crosses it (Magrina et al. 2022). GTEx bivalves the uterus
  along the endocervical canal, from the external os to the fundus, and
  samples the ectocervix, squamous, and the endocervix, glandular (GTEx
  Portal). *Consequence:* the cervix is drawn so, opened along its canal as
  GTEx opens it, its back half seen from the front ("The cervix" below).
- **The epididymis.** Anatomy plates show it on the right testis seen from
  its lateral side, the tunica vaginalis laid open (Gray 1918, Fig. 1148),
  and cut down its length with the testis to show the ducts (Fig. 1149).
  It is a long, narrow, flattened body on the lateral edge of the testis's
  back, its head enlarged at the upper end, its tail pointed at the lower,
  running on as the ductus deferens, its body between, the sinus of the
  epididymis a pouch between the body and the testis. Twelve to fifteen or
  twenty efferent ductules leave the rete testis, perforate the tunica
  albuginea and, exceedingly convoluted, form the coni vasculosi, which
  together make the head, each cone a single duct; opposite their bases
  they open into one duct whose convolutions make the body and the tail,
  over 6 m long unravelled and wider as it nears the ductus deferens,
  held together by areolar tissue and bands of fibrous tissue. The
  ductus deferens begins at the tail, at first very tortuous, and rises
  behind the testis and medial to the epididymis into the cord, with the
  testicular artery and the pampiniform plexus; the tunica albuginea is
  bluish-white; the testis 4 to 5 cm long, 2.5 broad and 3 from front to
  back, its upper end forward (Gray 1918). In man the proximal epididymis
  is formed by the efferent ducts alone (Sullivan et al. 2019), which join
  the epididymal duct near the border of the head and the body (Saitoh et
  al. 1990); 14.7 efferent ducts on average, 3.0 m long together, fill most
  of the head, and the epididymal duct is in segments parted by little or
  no connective tissue (Nakata et al. 2024). On ultrasound in 248 healthy
  fertile men the head is at most 12 mm and the tail at most 6, the vas
  deferens at most 4.5, the testis about 17 ml (Lotti et al. 2021); the
  vas deferens 1.5 to 2.7 mm thick (Middleton et al. 2009). GTEx samples no
  epididymis. *Consequence:* the epididymis is drawn so, on its testis from
  the side, cut down its length ("The epididymis" below).
- **The gallbladder.** Anatomy plates show it laid open from the front
  with its ducts, its front wall cut away (Gray 1918, Fig. 1095, after
  Spalteholz). It is a conical or pear-shaped musculomembranous sac, 7 to
  10 cm long and 2.5 cm broad at its widest, holding 30 to 35 c.c.: its
  fundus down, forward and to the right, its body and neck up and back to
  the left, the neck curving on itself like an S and constricted where it
  meets the cystic duct. Its coats are serous, over the fundus whole but
  under the body and neck alone, fibromuscular and mucous, the mucosa
  raised into minute rugae and, opposite the neck, into oblique folds, a
  sort of spiral valve; the cystic duct, about 4 cm long, has five to
  twelve crescentic folds; the hepatic duct comes down about 4 cm to meet
  it at an acute angle, and the common bile duct runs on about 7.5 cm; the
  cystic artery, usually from the right hepatic, runs down along the neck
  (Gray 1918). On ultrasound it is under 10 by 4 by 4 cm, its wall under 3
  mm, and Hartmann's pouch is found in 4.7 to 52% (Lucius et al. 2025);
  its wall has no muscularis mucosae and no submucosa, its perimuscular
  connective tissue running on into the liver's (Rana et al. 2025); the
  cystic duct is 4.21 mm across and joins the hepatic duct most often on
  its right, at its middle level (Plutecki et al. 2026); the cystic artery
  comes from the right hepatic in 86%, is 21.3 mm long and mostly over 1
  mm across (Triantafyllou et al. 2025). GTEx samples no gallbladder.
  *Consequence:* the gallbladder is drawn so, laid open from the front with
  its ducts ("The gallbladder" below).
- **The intestines.** Anatomy plates show them in place from the front,
  the greater omentum taken away, the small intestine's coils framed by
  the colon, and the cecum opened to show the ileocecal valve (Gray 1918,
  Fig. 1075). The jejunum, the upper two-fifths past the duodenum, is
  about 4 cm across, thicker and of a deeper colour than the ileum, 3.75
  cm, whose folds disappear toward its lower end; the terminal ileum rises
  out of the pelvis over the right psoas to open into the large
  intestine's medial side. Peyer's patches, twenty to thirty, 2 to 10 cm
  long, lie lengthwise, largest and most numerous in the ileum, on its
  wall away from the mesentery. The colon arches round the coils: the
  cecum, 6.25 cm long and 7.5 broad, the ascending colon, the transverse
  colon with its downward curve, the descending colon and the sigmoid loop
  in the pelvis; three bands about 12 mm wide gather its sacculi, and fat
  hangs from it in small pouches. The appendix averages 8.3 cm and opens
  below and behind the ileocecal opening, whose two lips run on as the
  frenula (Gray 1918). On CT the colon narrows from the cecum, 35.8 mm, to
  the sigmoid, 17.4 (Haraikawa et al. 2023). GTEx takes the small
  intestine from its most distal part, the thickened mucosa with Peyer's
  patches just short of the ileocecal valve (its Tissue Harvesting Work
  Instruction, 4.3.6.21). *Consequence:* the intestines are drawn so, in
  place from the front, a window cut at the ileocecal junction ("The
  intestines" below).
- **The lungs.** Anatomy plates show them in place from the front, the
  trachea coming down between them (Gray 1918, Fig. 970). The trachea is
  about 11 cm long and 2 to 2.5 cm across, ringed by sixteen to twenty
  cartilages each about 4 mm deep, and divides at the upper border of the
  fifth thoracic vertebra; the right bronchus is wider, shorter and more
  upright, about 2.5 cm, the left nearly 5 cm, passing beneath the aortic
  arch with the left pulmonary artery above and then in front of it. At
  the left hilum, a little above the middle of the mediastinal surface,
  the artery lies above, the bronchus below it, the veins lowest, the
  upper vein in front. The left lung's anterior border has the cardiac
  notch, the pericardium bare in it; its fissure crosses the posterior
  border about 6 cm below the apex and reaches the lower border a little
  behind its front end, the upper lobe above and in front of it, the lower
  lobe taking most of the base; the right lung is 2.5 cm shorter and
  broader, its horizontal fissure at the fourth costal cartilage. The
  surface is marked into polyhedral lobules, those under the pleura the
  larger; the larger bronchi carry irregular plates of cartilage (Gray
  1918). The bronchi are 12.2 mm across at the main bronchi, 8.3 at the
  lobar, 5.6 at the segmental, then 4.5, 3.5 and 2.8 (Weibel 1963); the
  main bronchi part at 77 ± 13° on CT (Karabulut 2005); the pulmonary
  trunk is 25.1 ± 2.8 mm (Truong et al. 2012); a secondary lobule is 1 to
  2.5 cm across (Webb 2006; Radiopaedia). GTEx samples the lung from the
  "inferior segment of left upper lobe, 1 cm below the pleural surface"
  (GTEx Portal), the lingula's lower segment. *Consequence:* the lungs are
  drawn so, in place from the front, the left one cut open where GTEx
  samples it ("The lungs" below).
- **The ovary.** Anatomy plates show it behind the broad ligament spread
  out, beside the uterus, its tube arching over it (Gray 1918, Fig. 1161),
  and cut through to show its follicles (Fig. 1163, after Schrön). Each
  ovary is about 4 cm long, 2 cm wide and 8 mm thick and weighs 2 to 3.5
  g; its uterine end, narrower than its tubal, is held to the uterus by
  the ligament of the ovary, a rounded cord; to its tubal end come the
  ovarian fimbria and the suspensory ligament with the ovarian vessels;
  its mesovarian border is straight and its free border convex, and the
  tube runs up along the one, over the tubal pole and down the other.
  Under its surface lie the follicles in their earliest condition, the
  cortical layer; larger ones lie deeper toward the medulla, the vascular
  stroma about the hilum, which has none; a ripening follicle's granulosa
  gathers into the discus proligerus about the ovum; a corpus luteum is
  its lining thrown into folds once the ovum has gone, its yellow cells
  later merging with the stroma (Gray 1918). The smallest follicles'
  oocytes have nuclei 14 to 23 µm across (Westergaard et al. 2007); in
  adults the primordial and primary follicles lie 460 to 500 µm under the
  surface and the secondary 640 µm (Haino et al. 2018). A dominant
  follicle is selected in the mid-follicular phase (Baerwald et al. 2012)
  and is 21.4 mm across at ovulation, from 17.4 to 27.0 (Bakos et al.
  1994); the last corpus luteum can still be seen through the next
  follicular phase (Baerwald et al. 2005); an ovary of the reproductive
  years holds about 7 mL and six antral follicles (Jabbar et al. 2026).
  GTEx samples "most normal regions of the left ovary" (GTEx Portal), in
  aliquots 10 by 10 mm and at most 4 mm thick (its Tissue Harvesting Work
  Instruction, 4.3.6.27.2). *Consequence:* the ovary is drawn so, the left
  one cut through its thickness, its tube arching over it ("The ovary"
  below).
- **Histology.** Haematoxylin stains nuclei and ribosome-rich cytoplasm;
  eosin stains protein; fat, mucus and lumens take neither. A ×40 objective
  under a standard eyepiece shows a field about half a millimetre across.
  *Consequence:* the tissue is that field, each tissue's architecture laid
  as the textbooks draw it (Junqueira's Basic Histology; Ross and Pawlina,
  Histology: A Text and Atlas), and each part by the stain it would take.
  It is drawn in the app's neutrals rather than in the stains' pink and
  purple (the user's choice of 2026-10-14): what takes stain lit, the
  denser the brighter, what takes none the ground.
- **A red cell has no nucleus.** It loses it as it matures in the marrow, in
  an island of erythroblasts round a macrophage (Chasis and Mohandas 2008).
  *Consequence:* where the Atlas's cell is a red cell, the zoom lands in an
  erythroblast of an island, and the card says "no nucleus".
- **The cell.** The Atlas images a cell in four channels: the nucleus blue,
  microtubules red, the endoplasmic reticulum yellow, the protein green
  (Thul et al. 2017). *Consequence:* the protein is placed where the Atlas
  finds it, brighter at its main locations. The channels' colours are not
  kept: the cell is sculpted in the neutrals, and the protein is its one
  accent.
- **The nucleolus.** The nucleus's densest body, where ribosomes are
  made, has no membrane: it is a condensate of three layers, pale fibrillar
  centres each in a rim of dense fibrillar component, in a granular bulk,
  round the ribosomal genes of the five acrocentrics (Lafontaine et al.
  2021; McStay 2016). *Consequence:* it is drawn lobed, not round, its edge
  soft, near black against the chromatin round it, and the acrocentrics'
  territories round it. Its layers are left out: it is one plain dark body
  (the user's choice of 2026-10-16, to keep the picture simple; its
  fibrillar centres showed once it was some 20 pixels across until then).
  A protein in the nucleoplasm glows round it, as immunofluorescence shows
  one.
- **Chromosome territories.** In interphase each chromosome keeps to a
  territory of its own, and territories are ordered from the centre of the
  nucleus to its edge by gene density: chromosome 19 toward the centre, 18 at
  the edge (Croft et al. 1999; Boyle et al. 2001). Painting each chromosome
  its own colour shows them (Speicher et al. 1996; Schröck et al. 1996).
  *Consequence:* 46 territories, sized by their DNA and placed in that
  order, each a step lighter or darker than its neighbour, the gene's
  chromosome in the accent and its homologue faintly in it: a nucleus holds
  two copies of every autosome, and of the X in a female cell, so the
  territory followed is one of two. A male cell's X has none, its second
  sex chromosome being the Y.
- **Bands.** A chromosome is seen whole only when it condenses to divide,
  some ten-thousandfold. Giemsa stain then bands it, and a band's name
  (11p15.5) is the address cytogenetics gives a gene (ISCN 2020). The bands
  follow the sequence itself, its GC-poor stretches staining dark (Niimura
  and Gojobori 2002). *Consequence:* the chromosome is a metaphase
  chromosome at that length, its band named, and nothing claims the gene is
  seen there.
- **A chromosome seen whole.** Its two sister chromatids lie side by side,
  joined at the centromere's primary constriction, each with a scaffold of
  its own (Paulson and Laemmli 1977; Poonperm et al. 2015). Each is
  chromatin folded into loops by condensins along its axis (Naumova et al.
  2013; Walther et al. 2018): in chicken cells, loops of about 80 kb nested
  in loops of about 400 kb, winding in a helix (Gibcus et al. 2018); in
  human cells, very large loops that overlap strongly (Beckwith et al.
  2025). The fibre itself is no regular coil but chromatin packed at varying
  density (Ou et al. 2017; Wako et al. 2020), and a good part of the
  chromosome's volume is a coat round the chromatin, not chromatin (Booth et
  al. 2016). A scanning micrograph shows the surface as knobs and globules
  (Sumner 1991; Shemilt et al. 2014), and atomic force microscopy shows
  G-bands as ridges and grooves of the relief, not of colour (Hoshi and
  Ushiki 2001; Ushiki and Hoshi 2008). The bands run across both sisters at
  the same heights, as stacked layers would (Daban 2015). On an
  acrocentric's short arm, the stalk holds the ribosomal genes, and a silent
  one is as condensed as the arm round it (McStay 2016). *Consequence:* the
  chromosome has a micrograph's form: two sisters touching along a groove,
  knobbly with chromatin, the bands soft changes of tone, an acrocentric's
  short arm whole. Its surface is lit as the zoom's organs are, not as an
  electron image, and its coat is not drawn (see "Chromosome" below).
- **The centromere and the telomeres.** Kinetochores assemble on the
  centromere, which coincides with the primary constriction a karyotype
  shows; each is a plate about 250 nm wide and 80 nm deep against the
  centromeric chromatin, one to each sister, and the two face opposite
  poles once the sisters are bi-oriented (Musacchio and Desai 2017).
  Every human chromosome ends in clusters of TTAGGG repeats, which FISH
  finds at the telomeres of all of them (Moyzis et al. 1988): a metaphase
  chromosome, two chromatids, has four. *Consequence:* the centromere's
  neck is its own chromatin, the home helix's clay half way over the
  arms' stone, and each of the four ends is capped in its honey over a
  third of the way over it (the user's asks, 2026-10-19, 2026-10-23 and
  2026-10-26, when a fifth of the way read as too faint); the
  kinetochores are not drawn, the neck standing for the centromere.
- **A gene by FISH.** A probe hybridised to a metaphase chromosome shows as
  a spot on each sister chromatid, at the same height on both (Trask 1991;
  Baumgartner et al. 1991): it places a gene to within about a band, and is
  about as big whatever the gene's length. *Consequence:* what is marked
  is what a probe can say, the gene's band: its chromatin, all round both
  sisters, is in the organs' home tone, plain, with no spot glowing over
  it (the user's asks, 2026-10-23 and 2026-10-26, so it takes the mark
  the other stops draw the gene's place in); nothing else on the
  chromosome is in the accent.
- **Illustration.** The pictures of chromosomes that read as current share
  one desaturated material, one glowing accent where the function is, and
  soft, knobbly surfaces lit from the side, without outlines or cut-away
  glass (for example EMBL's "Condensin shaping mitotic chromosomes by
  looping DNA", by Tobias Wüstefeld/Illustratoren.de). A zoom across scales
  reads best as one continuous travel, each level drawn in its own
  convention (Halladjian et al. 2020). *Consequence:* the chromosome's look,
  one warm stone lit as the zoom's organs are, its band the one accent,
  and the hand-overs either side of it.
- **The gene's place.** Its span is that of its MANE Select transcript on
  GRCh38 (Morales et al. 2022), and its bands the UCSC Genome Browser's
  cytoBand table.
- **Nucleosomes.** 147 base pairs wrap a core of eight histones (Luger et
  al. 1997; Davey et al. 2002), about one core every 200. At the start of a
  gene that is read there is a stretch free of them, with a positioned
  nucleosome just downstream (Schones et al. 2008). *Consequence:* the gene
  is a fibre of cores 200 bp apart, bare at its 5′ end.
- **The helix.** A base pair every 0.34 nm, about ten and a half to a turn.

## Visual language

- **One ground.** Every stop is on the app's own ground
  (`colorScheme.surface`), as every other screen is.
- **The accent follows the gene.** The theme's primary marks the gene and
  what it makes, at every scale: the organs its RNA is raised in (brighter
  where the level is higher), where its cell lives in the organ, its cell
  in the tissue, where its protein sits in the cell, its chromosome's
  territory, its band, and its gene on the map.
  Nothing else is in it.
- **Everything else is sculpted** in the app's warm neutrals, under one
  light from the upper left (`Sculpt`): a lit body from the surfaces up
  toward the bright ink, a rim where the light catches an edge, a soft
  shadow under the form, and fine line work in the quiet ink. A
  chromosome is drawn in the organs' colours (the user's choice,
  2026-10-26, after its soft blue read louder than every stop round it):
  its chromatin a warm stone, the organs' lit neutral warmed a little
  toward the home helix's honey, its centromere's neck the helix's clay
  and its ends its honey, each mixed into the stone, and the chromatin of
  the gene's band in the organs' home tone (the mark at 0.42 over
  `lit(0.2)`), from the accent's deep teal in shade toward the accent
  where lit; each from a
  ramp of its own in the theme (`ChromosomeColors`), mixed from the app's
  own colours, so it darkens and lightens in its own hue. Its arms are as
  quiet as the organs (a chroma of about 5), and nothing on it is more
  than half as saturated as the accent but its band's lit side.
- **Data keeps its colours.** The gene's parts are the walk's role colours,
  the bases the home helix's (`NucleotideColors.helix`), and blood the
  thymine red, the one red the app has.
- **Glows** are gradients and wide faint strokes, never a blur.
- **Names.** One plate a name, with a line to what it names, set clear of
  each other and 8 pt in from the edges. The card says the rest. The
  line is in the accent, but for a landmark's (the nucleolus, a
  chromosome's parts), which is in the quiet ink. A landmark's plate is set
  off what holds it, so it hides none of it: round the nucleus as a cell's
  names are round the cell, in the band above or below it nearer the
  nucleolus, out at the canvas's edge on the nucleolus's side, which is the
  left, the nucleolus lying toward the light (the user's ask, 2026-10-19);
  in a column against the canvas's edge either side of the chromosome,
  level with each part (see "Chromosome" below). The femur's names, its
  home's among them, are set in columns by the same rule
  (`columnAnchors`; "The bone marrow" below).
  Each keeps the place it has at rest through the steps either side, so it
  never jumps: an anchored name its anchor, or, where none of its anchors
  had room at rest, the place of the painter's own it took there.
- **Lines** are set in screen pixels, so they keep their weight at any
  magnification.

Every colour comes from `ZoomInks`, built from the theme:

| Ink | From | Drawn as |
|---|---|---|
| `ground` | `surface` | the ground at every stop |
| `raised`, `overlay`, `high`, `outline` | the surface containers and the outline | form in shade (`shade(t)`) |
| `lit(t)` | the outline lerped toward `onSurface` | form in the light |
| `label` | `onSurfaceVariant` | fine lines, quiet words |
| `text` | `onSurface` | rims, bright words |
| `mark`, `markDeep` | `primary`, `primaryContainer` | the gene and what it makes (`glow(a)`) |
| `shadow` | the ground three quarters of the way to black | shadows under forms |
| `blood` | the walk's thymine | red cells |
| `bases` | `NucleotideColors.helix` | the helix's bases, and a nerve in its guanine's honey (`nerve`) |
| `cds`, `utr`, `intron` | `AnatomyColors`' roles | the gene's parts |

`zoom_inks_test` holds that no colour in `lib/features/zoom/` is written as
a number, and that every ink is the theme's own or a mix of its own.

**Arrival beats.** Each stop plays a beat once as the depth comes to rest
on it; the DNA moves for as long as it is shown. Nothing moves under
reduced motion.

| Stop | Beat |
|---|---|
| Body | a band of the accent passes from head to foot in 1.1 s, then a ring pings from where the zoom goes into the followed organ (0.9 s) |
| Organ | a band of light passes over it (1.2 s), and the place it is sampled pings |
| Tissue, cell, nucleus | the zoom's cell, its nucleus, the gene's territory ping once (1.1 s) |

## One path per gene

The organ and the cell are not chosen by the app. The `locus` track's schema 2
(`pipeline/locus/` in the backend) chooses them, by one rule from the Human
Protein Atlas's readings:

- the organ is the tissue the RNA is highest in;
- the cell is one that lives there: the Atlas's own tissue cell type pair
  for that tissue, else the highest single cell type whose home it is, else
  none, and the tissue's own cells are drawn and said to be;
- a gene specific to no tissue goes to its top cell type's home;
- a cell with no nucleus lands in the one that has one.

Schema 1 took the top tissue and the top cell type each on its own, and seven
of the twenty went down into a cell of another organ: TNF's bone marrow into
microglia, myoglobin's skeletal muscle into thymic myoid cells. The bake's
README tabulates all twenty paths. A schema 1 payload still reads, with the
old rule.

## The camera

Each stop is a scene drawn in its own units: the width of the view at its
stop is one unit, and its origin is the middle of that view. Between two
stops the view narrows by the segment's factor, logarithmically in depth.

Each scene says where the next stop lies in it (its portal). The camera is
worked out from where the portal is on screen: the portal's offset from the
centre shrinks to nothing over the first 60% of a segment (a smootherstep),
and the centre follows. So the target never leaves the view, and every value
is a continuous function of depth. `zoom_domain_test` holds the portal on
screen and the view smooth across every segment of four genes, and holds the
scene two segments share to the same place at the stop between them.

- **Travel.** A segment's depth is its decades, but at least 0.6. The step
  from the nucleus to a long chromosome is only 0.07 decades: chromosome 1
  condensed is almost as wide as the nucleus, so it would otherwise flash by.
- **Units.** The view reads in metres down to the chromosome and half way
  past it, then in base pairs. The band, the gene and the DNA are placed in
  base pairs, projected to the screen in doubles, so no canvas transform
  carries the 10⁸-fold narrowing.
- **Framing.** A phone's canvas is about 1.45 times as tall as it is wide,
  so an organ taller than it is wide is framed by its height, and the
  chromosome's view is 0.9 of its condensed length, so it stands about
  three quarters of the canvas tall.

## The tissue

**The convention.** The tissue is a section as a pathologist reads one,
seen in the round field of an eyepiece 500 µm across (what a ×40 objective
shows), five sixths of the view wide. Each part is laid by the stain it
would take (`SlideInk`) and drawn in the zoom's inks, as though the section
were lit from the side on the app's ground (`paintTissueSlide`):

| Part (`SlideInk`) | Drawn |
|---|---|
| what takes no stain (`lamp`) | the ground |
| matrix and cytoplasm, palest to deepest (`eosin0` to `eosin3`) | the shade ramp, up into the light |
| ribosome-rich cytoplasm (`basophil`) | a cooled neutral |
| granules, striations (`granules`, `stria`) | lit, the striations as stripes 2.5 µm apart once they are wider than a pixel |
| cell borders (`border`) | the ground, stroked thin |
| fibres, cilia, tails (`fibre`) | the quiet ink, faint |
| lumens, fat, vessels (`clear`) | the ground, rimmed in light |
| nuclei, open to dense (`nucleus0` to `nucleus2`) | the quiet ink, brighter the denser |
| red cells | the thymine red |
| where the zoom's kind of cell lives (`home`) | the accent, faint |
| the zoom's cell | the accent: fill, glow and rim |

**One engine, a recipe an architecture.** `TissueSlide` lays a slide once,
in micrometres, from the tissue's recipe: each of the Atlas's 37 tissues has
one of 24 (`tissueAnatomy`). The engine (`SlideBuilder`) is seeds and the
Voronoi cells round them, relaxed so they pack as cells do, an epithelium set
cell by cell along a line, vessels, and fibres, all from one seeded
generator, so a tissue is the same slide every time. Sizes are the tissue's
own: a hepatocyte 24 µm, a fat cell 66, a seminiferous tubule 190 across.

| Recipe | Tissues | What is laid |
|---|---|---|
| acinar | pancreas, salivary gland | acini edge to edge, a pinpoint lumen; an islet among them, or a duct in its sheath, where the cell is of one |
| hepatic | liver | plates one or two cells thick running out from a central vein and branching, sinusoids with their lining cells and red cells |

## Smooth motion

**Flights.** A step is a flight in depth, a quintic from the depth and the
speed it has to the stop, arriving with no speed and no change of speed.
It takes 1.3 s plus 0.15 s a decade, held between 1.45 and 1.9 s: the
shortest steps need 1.4 s to keep every fade under 0.08 a frame at 60 fps,
and a long chromosome's turn into its band about 1.6. The step from the band
to the gene crosses 0.7 to 3.8 decades (1.45 to 1.87 s), and the step from
the gene to the DNA 1.9 to 5.3 (1.59 to 1.9 s). A step asked for
mid-flight starts the next flight at the speed the depth has, so it carries
straight on. A flick coasts 0.35 s at its own speed to choose its stop, and
a critically damped spring (stiffness 140) takes it there.

**Two layers.** The scenes are drawn again only when the depth moves. What
moves at rest (a beat, a helix turning, vesicles leaving a cell) is drawn
on a layer of its own (`ZoomScene.paintAmbient`) on an ambient clock that
runs only while a scene on screen wants it (`wantsClock`). A stop's beats
run on its dwell, the seconds since the depth came to rest there; under
reduced motion the dwell is long past every beat.

**Built ahead.** Each scene builds what it caches (`ZoomScene.prepare`)
before its first frame: one scene after each frame while nothing on screen
moves, the nearest the stop first, and the stop the zoom opens on holds its
beats until they are built. A step builds the scenes it will pass before it
moves, a pinch the two stops either way of where it starts. The tissue's
slide is the costly one: up to 60 ms on a Mac for the fallopian tube's
folds, which once fell in the last frame of the step to the organ.

**What `zoom_smoothness_test` holds.**
- Every protein's dive at 6,000 steps of depth, and every step either way
  at 60 fps, for all twenty.
- It fails on anything that appears, vanishes or changes by more than 0.08
  opacity in a step, or near the focus whose position's second difference
  passes 1.5 px. Further out, things fly outward faster the further they
  are as the view narrows, and the test checks for a break rather than a
  bound.
- A step asked for mid-flight takes over at the speed the depth has, never
  passes its stop, and comes to rest still.
- No two names overlap, and each keeps 8 pt from the edges, at 390 and 320
  wide; the band never sets two of its words over each other.
- As the step leaves the band, the gene lies on its mark on the strip, its

## Deliberate simplifications

- **The figure is a diagram.** Organs lie and overlap as the anatomogram
  draws them, from the front. Bone marrow is a femur, sampled in its upper
  end, where an adult keeps red marrow; a biopsy is taken from the pelvis.
- **An organ's anatomy is its kind's.** Islets, red marrow and tubules are
  drawn where general anatomy puts them, by the kind of organ and cell,
  not measured for the gene.
- **A pancreas from a plate.** Its shape is the same on every figure, only
  scaled to the outline the figure draws and sampled where the figure
  says. Its neighbours are those a plate shows with the stomach, the liver
  and the colon taken away. A million islets are a few hundred, at true
  size, placed by chance weighted to the tail; the ducts show only for a
  duct cell, through a cut cleaner than any dissection.
- **A testis from a plate.** One testis, the right, the same in every body,
  and seen from its side though the body is seen from the front. Its cut
  shows whole tubules coiling in their lobules, laid by rule, where a real
  cut would show their cut ends; the tissue stop shows those. The
  epididymis's head is lifted off it, as a plate lifts it, so the ductules
  show.
- **A femur from a plate.** One femur, the left, the same in every body but
  for its lean, and a young adult's marrow in it whatever the age. Its
  nutrient canal is drawn in the cut, though it pierces the linea aspera
  behind it. At the stop its trabeculae are every fourth line of the
  lattice; the true lattice comes in only as the view closes on the bone.
- **A parotid for a salivary gland.** Whatever gland the Atlas reads, it is
  drawn as the left parotid, in place; its lobules are 3 mm across, a size
  no study gives.
- **One brain, from a template.** The brain is the average of 152 adult
  brains, the same in every body. A cell of the brain lives in its cortex
  unless its tissue is the choroid plexus: S100B's Bergmann glia, of the
  cerebellum, and B3GAT1's oligodendrocytes, of the white matter, are drawn
  in the cortex too.
- **An eye that reads.** One eye, the right, turned so its cornea faces
  left. Its retina, true to its thickness, is a hairline at the stop, so a
  halo a pixel wide is drawn round it.
- **A heart in one cut.** No one plane holds the four chambers and the great
  vessels, the right ventricle lying in front of the left and the great
  arteries crossing: the cut is a composite, its long axis more upright than
  a heart lies, as the textbooks draw it.
- **A thigh for skeletal muscle.** Skeletal muscle is the front of the
  thigh, gone into in its vastus lateralis, whatever muscle the Atlas reads;
  its vasti run from the trochanter to the patella as the plates draw them,
  longer than measured.
- **A kidney, mirrored.** One kidney, drawn as a right kidney and mirrored
  for a left; its cut shows a quarter of the glomeruli a real cut would.
- **A liver and a stomach, composites.** No one plane holds the liver's vena
  cava, behind the portal vein, and its gallbladder, in front of it; the
  stomach's and the esophagus's folds are laid by rule, as a plate lays
  them.
- **Skin from one place.** Every skin is the left leg's, below the knee,
  where GTEx takes it, whatever the Atlas reads; two hairs stand on its top
  where a leg has more; no Pacinian corpuscle is drawn, Gray placing them on
  the palm, the sole and the genitals.
- **A thyroid without its pyramidal lobe**, which fewer than half of people
  have, and **an adrenal without its vena cava**, which would hide the right
  gland.
- **A pituitary on the brain's template.** Its lobes are the brain's own, so
  the two stops agree, a little larger than the MRI measures they sit
  beside.
- **A tube at a tube's length.** The anatomogram's arch is longer for its
  box than a tube is: the tube, and the figure round it, are drawn at 0.66
  of the size its outline grows to.
- **One placenta, at term**, a block of it, whatever the pregnancy; its
  card's thickness is a placenta's in utero, thicker than one delivered.
- **Fat from the abdominal wall.** Adipose tissue is the subcutaneous fat
  beside the navel, for a visceral adipocyte too.
- **A bladder from a plate.** One bladder, the same in every body,
  part-filled whatever the gene. Its folds are laid by rule and its
  trigone seen foreshortened; the uvula vesicae, the prostate's middle
  lobe, a man's alone, is not drawn. The figure's uterus is named where
  the anatomogram draws its outline below the bladder, and the
  anatomogram's smooth muscle, the bladder's own wall, is not drawn round
  it.
- **A breast at rest, from a plate.** One resting adult woman's breast,
  the same on both figures (most of GTEx's breast samples are men's) and
  for every cell, a lactating one too. Its ducts are the modern count, nine
  systems and no sinuses, five drawn in the dissected half; their courses,
  forks and widths (1.2 mm at rest), the lobes' shapes and the lobules'
  clusters are laid by rule, the lobules far fewer than a section counts.
  Its axillary tail, which the anatomogram does not draw, comes up with it.
- **A cervix from a plate.** One adult cervix, the same for every protein,
  its os small and round as Gray words it and its transformation zone round
  the os on the vaginal part. Its front half is taken away with the
  anterior lip and fornix; its folds, crypts and four cysts are laid by
  rule; no vein, nerve or ligament is drawn, and the ureters lie beyond the
  view. No cell the Atlas names lives in the cervix, so it has one home,
  the ectocervix, and the zoom goes on to its squamous epithelium whatever
  the gene.
- **An epididymis from a plate.** One adult's right epididymis, the same
  for every protein, cut down its length in the plane it is seen in while
  the testis beside it stays whole, as if the cut ran through the
  epididymis alone. Ten of its fifteen or so cones are in the cut, shared
  out alike along the head; its duct's profiles and septa are laid by
  rule, at sizes no source gives; no appendix, no ductulus aberrans, no
  paradidymis, no other epididymis. One site, where the efferent ductules
  have opened into the duct, for a cell of either.
- **A gallbladder from a plate.** One adult's gallbladder, the same for
  every protein, laid open as Gray's plate lays it, its honeycomb's meshes
  one size everywhere and its folds laid by rule; its serosa drawn all
  round, though it covers the body and neck only beneath; no stone. One
  home, its mucosa, for the one cell the Atlas houses there.
- **Intestines from a plate.** One adult's intestines, the same for every
  protein, in place as an atlas shows them: nine coils laid by hand, far
  fewer than the small intestine's length holds, their ends turning under
  one another into the shade; no mesentery and no vessels; the colon's
  sacculi and fat laid by rule. One site, the terminal ileum's Peyer's
  patch, for every cell: the slide is the small intestine's villi
  whatever the home.
- **Lungs from a plate.** One adult's lungs, the same for every protein:
  the left lung's cut shows its lobes as seen from the front and its
  hilum's bronchi and vessels together, a composite no single plane cuts;
  its branching past the segments is laid by rule, the lower lobe's
  bronchi only cut across, the heart and great vessels left to the
  figure. One site, the lingula's, for every cell: the alveolar slide
  follows for an airway cell too.
- **An ovary from a plate.** One ovary of the reproductive years, the same
  for every protein, at one moment of its cycle as Schrön's section draws
  it: a follicle near ripeness and the last cycle's corpus luteum
  together, five small antral follicles and three corpora albicantia, the
  earliest follicles a dust under the surface far sparser than the
  reserve; no cyst. The tube is the fallopian tube's own drawing, whole
  here, but cut short over the ovary's uterine third. One site, in the
  free border's cortex, for every cell: the slide is the ovary's stroma,
  an oocyte or a granulosa cell laid in it.
- **One field a tissue.** Wherever in the organ it is cut, and for every
  gene that goes there with the same kind of cell. "Lymphoid tissue" is a
  lymph node, and skin has no keratin layer of its own.
- **A section in the app's light.** Each part is laid by the stain it would
  take but drawn in the neutrals, with no chromatin texture and no
  variation in section thickness.
- **A cell of its kind.** The cell is its kind's shape and size, not a
  measured cell, and the accent fills the compartments the Atlas names
  rather than reproducing its image.
- **Territories are placed, not measured.** Their order from centre to edge
  is the literature's; their places are drawn. A male figure's nucleus holds
  an X and a Y, a female's two X.
- **A dividing chromosome in a resting nucleus.** The chromosome is seen
  condensed although the cell is not dividing: the territory condenses into
  it. Nothing on screen says so.
- **Textbook packing.** Nucleosomes are drawn every 200 bp from 40 bp into
  the gene, not where they sit on this gene.
- **One body.** A gene of tissues both sexes have is shown the female
  figure, unless more of its tissues are the male's alone.
- **A micrograph's form in colours of its own.** A scanning micrograph
  shows relief, not stain or colour: the chromosome keeps its form, its
  globules laid by rule from the chromosome's own seed, not imaged, and
  coloured as a drawing is, quietly, in the organs' colours: chromatin a
  warm stone, the centromere clay over it, the telomeres honey,
  the gene's band in the home tone (the user's picture of 2026-10-23 and
  their choice of 2026-10-26), its bands faint changes of the stone's
  value. The coat round its chromatin (Booth
  et al. 2016) is not drawn: as a faint haze it read as a washed-out
  surround (the user, 2026-10-22).
- **A probe's place.** The band in the accent is the gene's band whatever
  the gene's length, and two rows of globules at the least: it says where
  the gene is, to within its band, not how long it is.
- **Caps for telomeres.** A telomere is a run of repeats far too short to
  see at this scale: each cap says where one is, as the accent says where
  the gene is, not how long it is.
